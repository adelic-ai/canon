Are These Two Rules the Same?

A bottom-up derivation of why detection needs the Semantic Web.

on 3,748 real detection rules · everything reproducible from the corpus

The Semantic Web has an explanation problem. It is always pitched from the top down — a global web of machine-readable meaning — and the pitch never lands, because nobody can see what they would build. So let's not pitch it. Let's take one small, concrete question, push on it honestly, and watch the famous constructions — concept lattices, graded relations — appear not as theory but as the only answers left standing.

The question is this: are these two detection rules the same?

It sounds trivial. It is not. By the end you will have rediscovered FCA and SKOS — two acronyms from the Semantic Web stack — without ever being told to adopt them, because a pile of real rules will have forced them on you. The point is not to sell the Semantic Web. It is to show that you re-derive its pieces the moment you take meaning seriously on real data — which is the only honest reason to want them.

A note on scope, stated up front so it can't drift: this justifies the constructions — explicit concept structure and graded relations. It does not argue for the whole W3C tower (reasoners, triple stores, the linked-data cloud). "Why you need these ideas," not "therefore adopt everything."

1What a rule is, just enough to be concrete

The corpus is Sigma, the open standard for detection rules; we have 3,748 of them in a local copy. A rule reads fields off a log event and combines tests with boolean logic. Stripped to essentials:

detection:
  selection:
    Image|endswith: '\mimikatz.exe'
  condition: selection

That single test is an atom, and it has three parts: a field (Image), a predicate (endswith — how to compare), and a value (\mimikatz.exe). A rule is a boolean combination of atoms (and/or/not). And the predicate vocabulary is small — almost entirely string operations:

Hold onto that smallness; it returns at the end as the crux. First we try, and fail, to answer the question the easy way.

2The naive answer: are the texts equal?

The first instinct is to compare the rules as text. Two YAML files; are the bytes equal? This fails immediately, because the same detection can be written many ways:

# rule A            # rule B — same detection
  selection:           selection:
    Image|endswith:      CommandLine|contains: 'mimikatz'
      - '\mimikatz.exe'      Image|endswith: '\mimikatz.exe'
    CommandLine|contains:
      - 'mimikatz'
  condition: selection   condition: selection

Reordered keys, list-vs-inline, a stray space — byte-different, behaviorally identical. Text equality answers "different," and it is wrong. We need to compare what a rule is, not how it was typed. So we abstract away from the syntax and compare structure.

3Compare structure — and structure is already graded

Forget the text; compare what the rules are. And here is the thing the naive question hides: the honest answer is never just "same" or "different." Two rules can be identical; one can be a special case of the other (it adds a condition); they can overlap; they can be disjoint. The relationship between two rules is graded — it comes in degrees of generality.

Two pieces of the Semantic Web are exactly the machinery for that, and they are not a sequence of coarse-then-fine — they are two faces of the same structure:

  • FCA (Formal Concept Analysis) is the method: feed it objects (rules) and attributes, and it derives a concept lattice — and a lattice is already a hierarchy of more-general / more-specific. The order between concepts is broader/narrower; FCA hands you the grades, not just buckets.
  • SKOS (Simple Knowledge Organization System) is the vocabulary: it names those relations — exactMatch, broadMatch, narrowMatch, closeMatch.
FCA derives the graded structure; SKOS expresses it. FCA's lattice order and SKOS broader/narrower are the same relation seen from two sides — one computed from the data, one written down. You don't go "FCA, then SKOS for grades." You use FCA to find the relations and SKOS to say them. The instant you compare structure honestly, you are doing both.

4The one real knob: what you feed it

FCA needs attributes, and the whole result turns on which ones you pick. Take the naive choice — a rule's field-set: "which fields does it read?" It collapses the syntactic noise nicely, and for genuine duplicates it works. But it is value-blind, and on real data that bites hard. In the macOS slice of the corpus, twenty distinct detections — AppleScript run through osascript, base64 -d, touch to backdate a file, chflags to hide one, csrutil disable to turn off System Integrity Protection — all read the same two fields, Image and CommandLine:

20 distinct detections → 1 concept. Same field-set, twenty different values. A field-set key folds all twenty into one and calls them "the same rule." They are not. The key threw the values away.

Crucially, this is not a flaw in FCA or SKOS — it is a flaw in the attributes. Feed FCA richer attributes and the over-collapse vanishes. Include the values and the result sharpens — the twenty become twenty concepts, and across the whole macOS slice the value-aware key tells all seventy-five rules apart. And include the filters, too. Sigma's most common idiom is a positive selection wrapped in allowlists; here is a real rule at proc_creation_win_susp_parent_execute_itself.yml:

detection:
  selection:
    Image|fieldref: ParentImage          # a process whose image == its parent's
  filter_main_path:
    Image|startswith: ['C:\Program Files\', 'C:\Program Files (x86)\']
  filter_main_original_fn:
    OriginalFileName: ['Cmd.Exe', 'PowerShell.EXE', ...]
  filter_main_product:
    Product: ['Microsoft Office', 'Firefox', ...]
  condition: selection and not 1 of filter_*

The filters are not hidden — they are sitting right there in the rule, and there is nothing stopping you from feeding them to FCA. Tag each clause by polarity — positive selection clauses and negative filter clauses — and put them all in the attribute set. Both kinds narrow the rule (a positive clause says "must match more," a filter says "must match less"), so the lattice order — more clauses, of either polarity, is narrower — lines up exactly with SKOS narrowMatch. Now two rules with the same selection but different allowlists are correctly distinct, and "this rule is a stricter version of that one" is a relation the lattice states.

One subtlety the same example quietly settles. That selection clause, Image|fieldref: ParentImage, compares a field to another field, not to a literal value — every other predicate is field-to-value; fieldref is field-to-field, a relational attribute. It still goes straight into the formal context; it is simply a different kind of attribute (a field-pair), not a value clause. "Structure can't represent that" is never the obstacle — the only question is which kind of attribute each predicate becomes.

The lesson of the over-collapse is not "structure can't see the values or the filters." It can — you just have to include them. The real knob is attribute granularity: field-sets are too coarse; values-plus-filters are right. Same FCA, same SKOS — a richer formal context.

And this is not a macOS quirk. The same value-blindness bites in every Sigma product: across all sixteen with enough rules to measure, the value-aware key always carves more concepts than the field-set key — most sharply in Windows, where a single field-set concept swallows 903 distinct detections that the content key correctly tells apart, 903 → 903. macOS is a representative case, not an extreme one.

5The seam: even complete structure isn't behavior

So include everything — every field, every value, every filter, the whole boolean condition. You now have the most faithful structural description of a rule you can write. And it is still not what the rule does. Two reasons, and neither can be closed by adding more structure:

Semantic equivalence. Different structures can catch the same thing. Image|endswith: '\mimikatz.exe' and CommandLine|contains: 'mimikatz' are different fields, different predicates, different values — distinct by every structural key, however rich — yet they fire on the same events. The relation is sound in one direction only: identical structure guarantees identical behavior, but the converse fails. Structure can't see the equivalence because it is a fact about what the predicates mean over data, not about how they're spelled — and the predicate vocabulary is so small that there are always many spellings of one catch.

You can watch this happen. Feed the value-aware key all seventy-five macOS rules and it returns seventy-five distinct concepts and zero exact-match classes — it finds not a single pair of synonyms. Not because there are none, but because the only sameness that matters here — same catch, different spelling — is precisely the kind structure cannot see. And it is no small-sample artifact: across every Sigma product the value-aware key's concept count equals the rule count exactly — not one structural synonym in the whole corpus.

Data-relativity. "What a rule catches" is relative to the log. Two rules can be behaviorally identical on one dataset and divergent on another — an allowlist excludes something present in one environment and absent in the next. A structural comparison is data-free; it cannot give you a data-relative answer.

No structural key — field-sets, full content signatures, SKOS grades — can tell you what a rule catches. To know that, there is exactly one move left: run it. And running it needs a detector, real events, and a notion of "fired on the same instances." That is a different machine, and a different question — and it is where part two begins.

6So — why do we need the Semantic Web?

Because if you take one ordinary question — are these two things the same? — and refuse to lie about the answer on real data, you are walked, step by forced step, into its constructions. Text equality fails, so you compare structure — and structure is graded, which is FCA deriving a lattice and SKOS naming its relations. The first attributes you try over-collapse, so you make the attributes richer — values, filters, polarity — and the grades come right. None of it was abstract. Each piece was the cheapest tool that survived contact with 3,748 real rules.

That is the honest case for the Semantic Web, and the only one worth making: you do not adopt it as a vision; you rediscover its pieces, because the problem of meaning — what is the same, what is related — forces them on anyone who works at the level of data and won't fake the answers.

And we have stopped one question short. We learned what a rule says, and proved it isn't what a rule does: structure is sound but incomplete for behavior, and behavior is data-relative. The catch — what these rules actually fire on, and the four-valued logic you need to combine many of them honestly — is the behavioral axis, and it needs a detector. Part two runs the rules.
