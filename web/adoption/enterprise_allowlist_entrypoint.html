An enterprise entrypoint — mine the detections and their allowlists

Read the org's running Splunk and KQL correlation searches, harvest the allowlists they carry, and turn the org's own exclusions into enterprise-specific atoms — then entail ATT&CK and the kill chain on them.

canon · idea · 2026-06-21 · an exploration, not a committed design

This is an idea. A way into an enterprise that uses only artifacts they already run — no new sensors, no greenfield deployment. Written down to think with, not as a spec.

Every enterprise already runs a corpus of detections — Splunk SPL correlation searches, KQL/Defender/Sentinel analytics — and every one of those detections carries an allowlist: the exclusions the operators added so the rule stops firing on things they know are fine. That allowlist is a goldmine. It is the organization's own, hand-curated declaration of who and what is normal here.

What the allowlists give you

The exclusions are an inventory the org maintains without realizing it's an inventory. Pulled across the whole detection corpus, they name:

  • Device / machine accounts — the automated identities the environment runs on.
  • Service users — the broad-access non-human accounts that everything depends on.
  • Special-access human users — break-glass admins, privileged operators.
  • The infrastructure of "normal" — scanners, vuln assessors, jump hosts, backup agents.
These are exactly the high-value, hard-to-enumerate identities — the privileged and automated surface an attacker most wants to ride. The org has already written them down for you, one allowlist entry at a time. Read as a positive map instead of a suppression list, the allowlist becomes the watch set: the very accounts the SIEM tunes out are the ones whose off-profile behavior matters most.

The other half — the allowlist is also the blind spot

An allowlist doesn't only describe the privileged surface; it marks where detection has been deliberately switched off. That is its entire purpose — stop firing here. So the watch set is high-value twice over: it's the surface an attacker most wants to ride, and the surface the SIEM has agreed to stop watching. Whoever lands on an allowlisted identity is on the highest-value and least-watched ground in the same moment.

Read this way, the allowlist is the organization's own declaration of its control-gap — a map of exactly where detection has been turned off. A capable adversary wants that map; the org has been drawing it, one exclusion at a time. Watching the allowlist set for off-profile behavior puts eyes back precisely where they were removed — without re-enabling the noise the exclusion existed to silence. It is the "detection is the shadow of an absent or bypassable control" frame, read straight off the SIEM's own configuration.

Into the substrate → enterprise-specific atoms

Both halves go into canon: the detection logic (compiled to the IR) and the allowlist-derived identities. Out comes a set of enterprise-specific atoms — detection primitives shaped by this org's actual account taxonomy, not generic community rules. The org's own context is baked into what fires and what doesn't.

Then entail the frameworks

With enterprise-specific atoms in hand, entail MITRE ATT&CK and the kill chain on them — place each atom in technique-and-tactic space, and let the kill-chain structure connect them into trajectories. The result is a framework view grounded in this enterprise's detections and identities, not an abstract coverage chart.

Why it fits as an entrypoint

  • Uses what they already run. The detections and allowlists exist; reading them is low-friction and read-only on day one.
  • Enterprise-grounded from the start. The output is tuned to the org's real identity surface — the context that's normally tribal knowledge, surfaced from artifacts.
  • Plays to canon's strengths. Detections → IR, atoms, and framework entailment are the substrate's native moves; this just feeds them the richest enterprise-specific input available without instrumenting anything new.
One line. Walk in through the detections and their allowlists, turn the org's own exclusions into enterprise-specific atoms, and entail ATT&CK and the kill chain on what comes out — an entrypoint built entirely from what the enterprise already runs.
