Witness & check

One pattern runs under integer factoring, Shor's algorithm, and the semantic substrate. Naming it tells you exactly where an LLM belongs.

canon · session synthesis · 2026-06-09 · RSA / quadratic sieve / Shor → generator–validator → fold-certificate

A long thread that started at SignalForge's prime-coordinate viewing box and ended at "what if I built a semantic LLM" turns out to be one idea wearing several costumes. The idea: search for a witness is expensive and creative; checking a witness is cheap and sound. Keep those two jobs separate and you know what every component is for — including the model.

The asymmetry, in its purest form: RSA

An RSA public key is a modulus N = p·q (the "modulus" you see in a certificate) plus an exponent e. Breaking the key is factoring N: recover p, q and you compute the private exponent directly. The security is a one-way street:

multiply p·q → N      cheap        (forward / generate)
factor   N  → p,q     infeasible   (inverse  / solve)

And the tell-tale: once someone hands you p, verifying it is a single division. The hard direction is finding it; the easy direction is checking it. That gap is the whole of RSA.

Why the neighborhood of N doesn't help — and the viewing box says so

A natural instinct: look at N−1, N−2, …, the additive neighborhood, maybe read the factor structure off the p-adic coordinates. It fails, and the prime-coordinate viewing box renders why: adjacent integers have wildly unrelated coordinate columns. There is no continuity, no gradient, from one column to the next — because factoring is a multiplicative property and the integer line's adjacency is additive. The two structures are orthogonal.

Concretely, anything a smooth neighbor N−m tells you reduces to a congruence N ≡ m (mod d) — one equation in the product p·q, never separating the factors, and freely computable by reducing N directly. The productive neighborhood is not around N at all. It is around √N.

The neighborhood of √N: the viewing box is the sieve's matrix

The Quadratic Sieve harvests smooth values of Q(x) = x² − N for x near √N (small, hence likely smooth). Each smooth value is a relation x² ≡ ∏ pₙeₙ (mod N) — recorded as its prime-exponent vector. Stack the vectors, reduce mod 2 (because "perfect square" means "all exponents even"), and find a GF(2) null vector: a subset whose product is a square on both sides.

Those prime-exponent vectors, reduced mod 2, are the columns of the Neighborhood viewing box. The quadratic sieve's linear-algebra step is a hunt for a mod-2 null vector in prime-coordinate space. The seed concept isn't adjacent to factoring — it is the sieve's data structure.

QS and Shor converge on the same witness

Both champion algorithms — classical and quantum — have the identical endgame: manufacture a nontrivial square root of 1 mod N (an x with x² ≡ 1, x ≢ ±1), then take one gcd. They differ only in how they build it:

  • QS constructs it additively, from smooth relations, via GF(2) linear algebra in the prime-magnitude basis — the viewing box.
  • Shor reaches it through the order r of a random element (ar/2), found by period-finding — a Fourier transform in the period-dual basis.

Same target, two sides of a transform. And the witness itself is checkable in one gcd. The machinery — sieve or quantum — is the expensive, clever generator; the check is trivial and sound.

The pattern, named

Powerful search for a witness + cheap sound check of the witness. The search may be a sieve, a quantum circuit, a heuristic, a language model — untrusted, creative, as expensive as you like. The check is small, deterministic, and sound, and it is the trust anchor. Soundness lives in the checker, never in the searcher.

This is the same split as generator / validator, and it is the spine of the canon substrate: every artifact ships with the function that decides whether it is true. The valuation map (forward, generative) proposes; the certificate (gcd splits N; here is p) proves. clipclop computes the easy direction beautifully and is, by construction, a validator-shaped tool that cannot solve — exactly the right read.

Where this lands the LLM

The "mechanical reasoning" of semantic-cyber — the ≤k-monotone fold over the content-addressed DAG, the soundness proof per detection, SKOS subsumption, the FCA closure, the Belnap join — is the checker. It is symbolic, deterministic, and sound, and it runs fine on a laptop; it is bounded by correctness, not compute. An LLM is the searcher: probabilistic, fluent, unsound. Put the model in the checker's seat and every verdict becomes "the model said so" — which is the commercial-detection product the substrate is defined against. The differentiator is the certificate; an LLM verdict has no certificate, only a confidence score.

Measured, in this corpus: FCA agreed with LLM-style semantic judgment on only 4 of 12 edges, and the standing rule is default to the tool. The mechanical reasoner is already the better reasoner about the ontology. Don't hand it the reasoning workload.

"What if I made a semantic LLM?"

The strong version of the idea is right — provided it changes the searcher, not the line. Training a model on the substrate, or constraining its decoding to the typed grammar, makes it a far better generator and can provably own one slice of soundness:

  • Constrained / type-constrained decoding guarantees the output lands in the valid set — well-formed, on-vocabulary, schema-valid. That is a genuine soundness sub-property moved into generation.
  • But well-formed ≠ true. A type-correct detection DAG can still fail to detect the TTP or carry the wrong false-positive bound. That step is still the fold + certificate.

So a semantic LLM shrinks the generator/validator gap (far higher first-pass validity) but does not erase the generator/validator distinction. The difference was never how much the model knows; it is the kind of guarantee — "very likely true" and "true, here is the certificate" are different species of object, and no amount of semantic training converts the first into the second.

The marriage that actually works

Don't build a semantic LLM to be the reasoner. Build one that emits checkable certificates — witnesses — rather than verdicts. Then the model can be as creative as you like, because the cheap sound checker is the trust anchor. This is the LLM-prover + kernel pattern: the model writes the proof, a tiny sound kernel checks it; the model is untrusted, the kernel is the trust.

semantic LLM  →  fold-certificate (witness)  →  checker (kernel)  →  sound verdict
 untrusted          higher hit-rate of              cheap, deterministic,
 searcher           valid certificates              the trust anchor

Your guarantee_certificate.schema.json checker is the kernel; the semantic model just raises the rate of valid certificates it has to check. Generator/validator preserved; the generator is now semantic, constrained, and proof-carrying — and that is a real, ambitious job for a large local model.

The invariant, and the guard

  • Invariant: the witness stays cheaply, soundly checkable, and the checker stays outside the model. Make the search semantic — good. Move the line — never.
  • Guard: "semantic" is a representation upgrade. Representation ≠ the guarantee you want — structure doesn't fix recall, and a semantic latent space doesn't fix soundness. A better map is not a proof that you stand where the map says.
Factoring, Shor, the quadratic sieve, and the substrate are the same shape: an expensive search for a witness that a cheap sound check certifies. The model belongs on the search side of that line. The certificate is the asset; the checker is the substrate.
